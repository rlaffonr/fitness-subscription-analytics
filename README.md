Subscription Analytics — Analysis Summary

-Customer Cohort Analysis

Question: After how many months do customers typically cancel their subscriptions?

Based on the cohort retention matrix, most customer cancellations happen very early within the first 3 months of the subscription. Looking at the January 2024 cohort as an example (which has the most complete data, 156 customers at month 0):

By month 1, retention drops to ~85% (133 of 156)
By month 2, retention drops further to ~78% (122 of 156)
By month 3, there's a sharp cliff: retention falls to just 50% (78 of 156) the single steepest month over month drop in the entire curve

After month 3, the decline slows down considerably. Retention keeps decreasing gradually but stabilizes into a much flatter curve from around month 8–12 onward, settling around 30–35% of the original cohort by month 24.

Takeaway: the critical churn window is the first 3 months this is where the business loses roughly half of each cohort. If retention efforts (onboarding improvements, engagement campaigns, check-ins) are going to have the most impact, they should be concentrated in that early window, since customers who make it past month 3 tend to stick around much longer and the churn rate levels off significantly.

-Revenue Forecast

Question: What is the expected subscription revenue over the next 12 months? Does the revenue forecast show evidence of seasonality?

Based on the forecast visual, monthly subscription revenue is projected to grow from approximately $190K (the last actual data point, January 2026) to roughly $350K by around December 2026, following a steady upward trend.

The forecast shows:

Near-term (Q1 2026): revenue climbing from ~$190K toward ~$230K
Mid-term (mid-2026): continuing to rise into the $270K–$290K range
End of forecast window (late 2026): reaching approximately $310K–$350K, with a wider confidence band as the projection extends further out

Confidence interval: the shaded gray area (95% confidence interval) widens considerably toward the end of the forecast by December 2026, the plausible range spans roughly $240K to $470K, reflecting increasing uncertainty the further out the model projects.

Caveat worth noting: the forecast follows the strong upward trend visible in the historical data, but there's a sharp dip right before the forecast starts (revenue drops from ~$280K to ~$190K in the last actual month). This kind of abrupt drop just before the projection begins can sometimes distort a forecasting model's baseline it's worth confirming whether that final dip reflects a real business event (seasonality, a one-off issue) or a data completeness issue (e.g., the most recent month not yet fully closed out), since that will affect how much confidence to place in the forecasted trajectory.

-CAC vs. LTV

Question: How long does it take for the 2024 and 2025 cohorts to recover their customer acquisition cost?

Break-Even Analysis by Cohort

Both the 2024 and 2025 customer cohorts reach CAC break-even remarkably quickly within their own year of acquisition, rather than requiring an extended payback period.

2024 Cohort:

Total CAC: $501,536.80
Break-even reached: December 2024
The cohort recovers its full acquisition cost within the same calendar year it was acquired.

2025 Cohort:

Total CAC: $1,162,459.11
Break-even reached: December 2025
Consistent with the 2024 cohort, break-even occurs within the same year of acquisition.
Key Takeaway

Both cohorts demonstrate strong unit economics, recovering their acquisition cost in under 12 months a healthy payback period by most subscription business standards. This indicates that revenue per customer is scaling efficiently relative to acquisition spend across both cohorts.

One notable shift between cohorts is the increase in Total CAC more than doubling from $501K (2024) to $1.16M (2025). This could reflect either a larger customer base acquired in 2025 (a positive growth signal) or a rising cost per acquired customer (which would warrant closer review of acquisition channel efficiency). Segmenting CAC on a per customer basis, rather than as a cohort total, would clarify which of these factors is driving the increase and help determine whether the trend is sustainable as the business scales.
